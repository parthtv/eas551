Overview

This course consists of four parts. The first part will provide a primer for those who are curious about the physical mechanisms by which climate is determined, and by which climate change occurs. The treatment of these mechanisms will not be overly quantitative, and no prior knowledge of meteorology or atmospheric science is assumed. The second part will describe the projected consequences of climate change, and those that are already occurring; it will also familiarize students with how societies might adapt to these changes. The third part will explore (and critique) some of the tools that decision-makers use to quantify and compare the damages caused by these consequences. The final part of the course will discuss some of the technologies that could be deployed to stave off dangerous climate change.

Learning Objectives

  • Accurately describe the conclusions (and limitations) of literature on the causes and consequences of climate change, and know where to seek more detailed information.
  • Run simple climate models and offer high-level explanations of the relationship between their inputs and outputs.
  • Describe the consequences of climate change and different approaches to mitigating it.
  • Describe and apply the methods used to quantify the damages caused by climate change and the costs of mitigation, and demonstrate an awareness of the limitations of such methods.

Quizzes (55%): There will be a quiz on each Monday; there will be 12 quizzes and the lowest score will be dropped. Each quiz counts for 5% of the grade. Quizzes are in-class, on paper, and timed (15–20 mins). You can use written or printed notes but must close your computers. Any mathematical formulae or values of constants will be provided in the questions. You are free to use calculators.

Mid-term (20%): Based on the climate science component of the class. It will take place on October 28. The mid-term is in-class, on paper, and timed. Notes permitted, computers closed, calculators allowed.

Class presentations (25%): Please form teams of two before September 30 and update this spreadsheet, picking a presentation slot (log in with your U-M Google account). Pick a topic based on the syllabus. Before October 28, upload a 200-word abstract of your proposed presentation (5% of the total course grade); you will receive feedback on this proposal.

A few ground rules. For regrades, email a request explaining your reasons within a week of receiving the grade for that assignment. For presentations you work in pairs and may split the work as you see fit; by default both members receive the same grade (raise concerns with the instructor or GSI before the presentation). If you use AI for anything, say so and describe in a sentence or two how; learn its limitations (e.g., it cannot distinguish truth from falsehood). You are responsible for any errors AI introduces and will be graded accordingly.
